# Key policy — who signs what, and why the exciting path is the safe one

**Status: PROPOSED 2026-08-22. Nothing here is implemented yet.** It exists to be
argued with before code is written. H3 in `SECURITY_REVIEW_2026-08-07.md` is the
gap this closes.

## The threat this is actually designed against

Not key theft. The realistic failure for a one-person publisher is **the owner
signing something at midnight because it was interesting.** A policy that assumes
restraint from the person it protects is not a policy, it is a wish.

So the design rule is: **make signing cheap and safe, and make the dangerous claim
impossible to make by hand.**

## The two claims, deliberately separated

Today the catalogue index makes one undifferentiated claim, and the word `proved`
in it is attacker-authored text. Split it in two:

| claim | means | who may assert it |
|---|---|---|
| **publisher signature** | "the factory admitted these bytes" | Tony, freely — but only over what the gate passed |
| **`proved`** | "the factory's gate discharged this core" | **the gate only** — never a human, never a hand edit |

This is the whole trick. Signing stops being a dangerous act, because a signature
says only *we published this*, not *this is correct*. Publish a hundred interesting
things; none of them acquire a proof claim by being published.

**`proved` must carry its evidence or it is not written**: the ledger id, the unit
name, the toolchain digest that discharged it. A `proved` field with no ledger id is
refused by ghillie on sight — not warned about, refused. An entry with no `proved`
field is fine and normal; it means what it says, which is nothing.

## The keys — DECIDED 2026-08-22 (Tony)

**One publisher key. No offline root tier.** Tony's call, made with the trade-off
in front of him: *"accept the key risk, store it on here, back it up on the NAS."*
Recorded as a decision, not a default — the two-tier design was offered and
declined as disproportionate for a one-person publisher.

| key | home | job |
|---|---|---|
| **publisher** | Bill, `0600`, never in git | signs the catalogue index |
| **backup** | Synology `MagicRoundabout` (`Claude@192.168.0.43`), beside the existing `~/factory-backup` bundles | survives a dead laptop |
| **facade** | unchanged — travels at enrolment, TOFU-pinned | signs frames |

Ghillie pins the **publisher** public key at enrolment, the same road the facade
key already travels.

**The key is generated by Tony and never handled by the seat.** Same rule as the
Google OAuth client secret: a private key that passes through a transcript is a
leaked private key. The seat writes the tooling; the owner runs the keygen.

### What accepting this risk actually costs — stated plainly

- **Rotation is a re-pin event.** With no root to vouch for a successor, replacing
  the publisher key means every installed ghillie must pin the new one. Fine at
  today's scale; it gets expensive exactly when the catalogue succeeds. Revisit
  when there are more ghillies than you can telephone.
- **A leak has no revocation path short of that re-pin.** The offline root existed
  to make a leaked publisher key a Tuesday rather than an emergency. Declining it
  means a leak IS the emergency. Detection therefore matters more than it would
  have: the blotter is not decoration.
- **The NAS backup covers loss, not site loss.** Bill and the NAS normally sit in
  the same building, and the NAS is behind the powerline segment that took the
  whole estate down on 2026-08-12. It defends against a dead disk and a fat
  finger, which is most of what actually happens. It does not defend against fire
  or theft. If that gap ever matters, the existing offsite route is the answer,
  not a second key tier.

## The rules that survive enthusiasm

1. **Signing is gated, not available.** `make publish` refuses unless
   `~/.publish-gate-open` was touched inside the last 15 minutes — the same
   time-boxed door as the factory seat gate, which is already proven to work on
   this owner. A door left open past its window is itself a flag.
2. **Every signature is blottered.** Append-only, what was signed, when, and the
   digest. If you cannot remember signing it, the blotter can.
3. **Nothing is offered that the factory has not admitted** (Tony, 2026-08-22:
   *"they have to be provided by the factory or approved by the factory"*). Two
   roads in — forged here, or submitted from outside and passed — but ONE gate,
   and the signature attests that the gate saw it, never who wrote it.

   This is deliberately not an authorship rule. Authorship is the wrong test: it
   would bar the contributor economy the catalogue is designed around, and it
   would still let the owner wave through his own bad afternoon. Admission is the
   right test, and it is the same test the rest of the factory already applies.

   It is also the stronger version of the anti-enthusiasm property. Being excited
   about a stranger's clever ability is now harmless, because excitement cannot
   admit it — the gate does. There is no route where liking something is what
   gets it published.

   **What "approved" means, precisely.** Not "proved" — most of an ability is
   glass and there is nothing to discharge. It means the factory's deterministic
   checks ran and passed: the five-part contract holds, the archive is files and
   directories only, nothing carries an executable bit, the bounds hold, the
   opsec gate passed over every client-visible string. Where an ability contains
   a real core, `proved` is set SEPARATELY by the gate that discharged it, with
   its ledger id. Admitted and proved are two different words and the index must
   never blur them.
4. **The signing key never leaves its machine, and the root never joins one.**
5. **No emergency exceptions.** The interesting thing will still be interesting
   in the morning, inside the window.

## Rotation and revocation

The signed index carries `key_id`, `expires`, and a revocation list. Ghillie refuses
an expired publisher key, refuses a revoked one, and refuses an index whose signature
does not verify — loudly, never degrading to "unverified but installed anyway". A
refusal the owner can see is the asset; a silent downgrade is the thing this file
exists to prevent.

Root compromise is the unrecoverable case, and it is meant to be: that is why the
root lives offline and signs twice a year.

## Honest limits

- TOFU pinning trusts the first enrolment. A hostile door at first contact wins,
  and no key policy fixes that — it is a property of TOFU, stated rather than hidden.
- This protects the supply chain, **not** the correctness of what is inside a
  bundle. A signed, unproven ability is exactly as unproven as an unsigned one. The
  signature says who; only the gate says what.
- None of it is implemented. Written 2026-08-22 alongside H1/H2 and the first two
  links of H3, so the remaining decision is a decision and not a blank.
